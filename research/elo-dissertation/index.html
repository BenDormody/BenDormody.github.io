Evaluating Football Player 1v1 Ability Using Elo Rating Models

Middlesex University · 2026 · 17 pp · PDF · 1.5 MB
Read the PDF Download View code on GitHub

Abstract

Football clubs increasingly rely on data analytics for scouting, opposition analysis, and performance review — yet many of the indicators used for this work ignore the quality of opposition a player actually faced. The gap shows up most clearly in metrics for 1v1 duels and dribbles, which record only success or failure and treat every opponent as interchangeable: a player who repeatedly targets weaker defenders can end up with a better record than one who takes on stronger opposition, regardless of who is the better dribbler.

This dissertation builds separate attacking and defensive Elo ratings (base 1,000, K = 32) from StatsBomb open event data for the 2015/16 season — 1,140 matches and 37,759 attacker–defender duel pairs across the Premier League, La Liga, and Serie A — and compares them against traditional success-rate metrics, computing ratings independently within each league before pooling to test whether the patterns hold in aggregate.

In the pooled sample, Elo tracked success rate strongly (attacking r = 0.84, defending r = 0.90, both p < .0001) — but individual players diverged sharply between the two rankings. Jamie Vardy ranked 29th by Elo and 227th by success rate among 297 qualifying Premier League attackers; divergences reached 198 places. Attacking and defensive Elo were only weakly related within players (pooled r = 0.076), and success rate was shown to conflate ability with opposition difficulty in a position-dependent way — underrating strikers and wingers, overrating deeper roles. Elo emerges as a statistically robust, opposition-adjusted complement to success rate within a league.

Why it matters

For recruitment: a dribbler's raw success rate can be farmed against weak defenders; an Elo rating cannot. For opposition analysis: defensive Elo surfaces players who quietly beat elite attackers even when their raw numbers look unremarkable. The method needs only duel outcomes to compute, so it extends naturally to other inverse-metric pairs — pressing vs. ball-carrying, aerial duels, shooting vs. goalkeeping.
